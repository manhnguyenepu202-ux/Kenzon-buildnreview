Most people shop for a laser by wattage. That is the second question. The first is **wavelength**, because it decides which materials absorb the beam at all. A 70W diode laser and a 40W CO2 laser are not stronger and weaker versions of the same thing: they are different tools that are good at different jobs.

> **Key takeaways**
> - **Diode lasers (about 450 nm, blue)** are the cheapest and easiest way into wood, leather, card and dark acrylic. They can’t cut clear acrylic and can’t mark bare metal well.
> - **CO2 lasers (10,600 nm, infrared)** cut and engrave almost every organic material, plus clear acrylic, glass and stone. They need cooling, ventilation and some maintenance.
> - **Fiber lasers (1,064 nm)** are metal markers. They are fast and permanent on steel, aluminium and brass, and poor at wood and clear plastics.
> - Buy for the material you will use most, then pick the wattage.

## Why wavelength matters more than watts

A laser only does work where the material absorbs its light. Blue diode light at around 450 nm is absorbed well by dark, organic materials such as wood and leather, which is why a modest diode engraves plywood beautifully. The same light passes straight through clear acrylic and glass, and bounces off most bare metal.

CO2 light, at 10,600 nm, sits far into the infrared. Wood, paper, leather, fabric, rubber and every kind of acrylic absorb it strongly, and so do glass and stone surfaces, which is why a CO2 laser can frost a wine glass or engrave slate. Metal reflects it, so a CO2 laser can’t mark bare steel without a marking spray.

Fiber light, at 1,064 nm, is the opposite: metals absorb it well, which makes fiber the standard for engraving serial numbers, tools, tags and tumblers. Most wood and clear plastic do not absorb it well.

## The three types at a glance

| | Diode | CO2 | Fiber |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wavelength | ~450 nm (blue) | 10,600 nm | 1,064 nm |
| Best at | Wood, leather, card, dark acrylic | Wood, all acrylic, glass, stone, leather | Metals, some plastics |
| Can’t do well | Clear acrylic, bare metal | Bare metal | Wood, clear acrylic |

## Diode lasers: the right first laser for most people

If most of what you make is wood signs, leather patches, coasters and cut-out plywood shapes, a diode laser is the sensible choice. They are cheap, small, need no cooling water, and the enclosed models are safe enough for a spare room with ventilation.

Power has climbed fast. The [Sculpfun SF-A9](/en/review/sculpfun-sf-a9/) measured 41W of optical output in Hobby Laser Cutters’ test and cut 10 mm plywood in a single pass at 300 mm/min. At the small end, the enclosed [Creality Falcon A1C](/en/review/creality-falcon-a1c/) cut 5 mm basswood in one pass in 3DPrinting.com’s test and costs under $400.

**Where diodes struggle:**

- **Clear and light-coloured acrylic.** The blue beam passes through. You can cut black or dark acrylic, not clear.
- **Bare metal.** A diode can mark coated or anodised metal and can use marking sprays, but it will not engrave bare stainless steel the way a fiber laser does.
- **Thick material quickly.** Even a strong diode needs several passes on thick hardwood, and each pass chars the edge a little more.

A word on watts: some older diode listings quoted electrical input power, which made a 5W optical laser look like a 40W one. Reputable brands now quote optical output. If a listing is vague, assume the smaller number.

## CO2 lasers: the workhorse for makers who sell

A CO2 laser is what most small laser businesses end up with, because it handles the widest range of materials with clean edges. Clear acrylic is the classic example: CO2 cuts it with a polished, flame-finished edge that a diode simply can’t produce.

The cheapest route in is a K40-style machine. The [OMTech K40+](/en/review/omtech-k40-plus/) costs $579.99 and, in Go Laser Go’s tests, engraved clear acrylic, glass, slate and ceramic tile, and engraved a wood coaster in under five minutes. The price of entry is patience: you align mirrors by hand, the bed is only 8 × 12 inches, and the machine cuts thin sheet rather than thick stock.

Step up to a desktop machine like the [OMTech Polar Lite](/en/review/omtech-polar-lite-55w/) ($2,199.99) and you get autofocus, a camera, built-in water cooling and a pass-through slot. Cabinet machines such as the Pronto series add big beds and production speed, at the cost of a lot of floor space.

**What CO2 ownership really involves:**

- **Cooling.** Glass tubes are water-cooled. Some machines include a small reservoir; larger ones need a chiller, often sold as an add-on package.
- **Ventilation.** Cutting wood and acrylic makes a lot of smoke. You need to vent outside or use a capable fume extractor.
- **Tube life.** Glass tubes are consumables, usually rated in the thousands of hours. RF tubes last longer and cost far more up front.
- **Alignment.** Cheaper machines steer the beam with mirrors that drift and need occasional alignment.

## Fiber lasers: buy one when metal is the job

If you want to engrave knives, tools, dog tags, tumblers or business cards in metal, a fiber laser is the right tool, and it is dramatically faster than anything else. In CNX Software’s test of the [Creality Falcon T1](/en/review/creality-falcon-t1/), the fiber module marked three aluminium dog tags in about a minute and a half.

Most fiber lasers are **galvo** machines: mirrors steer the beam across a small field, often around 110 to 200 mm square, instead of moving a head across a large bed. That makes them fast but small.

Two things catch first-time buyers out:

- **Standard vs MOPA.** A standard Q-switched fiber laser, like the [OMTech Galvo 30W Split](/en/review/omtech-galvo-30w-split/), marks metal in black, white and grey. Colour marks on stainless steel need a MOPA fiber laser with adjustable pulse width, which costs more.
- **Eye safety.** Fiber light is invisible and dangerous to eyes. Open galvo machines need goggles rated for 1,064 nm, and a blue-light diode pair will not protect you.

## A simple way to decide

1. **Mostly wood, leather and card, on a budget?** Start with a diode. Enclosed if it lives indoors.
2. **Clear acrylic, glass, stone, or you plan to sell products?** Save for a CO2 laser and budget for ventilation and cooling.
3. **Metal is the main job?** Buy a fiber laser, standard for black-and-white, MOPA for colour.
4. **Want two of these?** Modular machines like the Falcon T1, or a diode plus a small fiber, often cost less than one machine that tries to do everything.

## The costs nobody puts in the headline price

Whatever you choose, budget beyond the machine: an air assist pump if it isn’t built in, a honeycomb bed, goggles for open machines, ventilation or a fume extractor, and software. LightBurn, the software most owners settle on, sells a $99 licence for GRBL-based diode machines and a $199 Pro licence that covers CO2 controllers. Those extras can add 20 to 30 per cent to a cheap laser’s real cost.

## The bottom line

Pick the wavelength first, then the power. A diode laser is the best first machine for most hobbyists, a CO2 laser is the most versatile tool for a small business, and a fiber laser is the only sensible way to mark metal at speed. If you match the laser to your material, almost any of the machines in our reviews will serve you well.
