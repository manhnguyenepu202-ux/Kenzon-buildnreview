Most filaments absorb moisture from the air, some of them surprisingly fast. When a damp strand hits a hot nozzle, the water inside turns to steam. The result is a print that strings, pops and fizzes, has a rough or bubbly surface, and breaks more easily than it should. Plenty of "my printer needs tuning" problems are really "my filament needs drying" problems. Below we cover how to tell when filament is wet, the temperatures to dry each material at, and the habits that stop it happening again.

## 1. Learn the Signs of Wet Filament

Wet filament gives itself away. Listen for **popping or crackling** from the nozzle while it prints, and watch for small **puffs of steam**. Look for **stringing** that your usual retraction settings used to prevent, a **rough, fuzzy or bubbly surface**, and **inconsistent extrusion** with gaps in thin walls.

The filament itself changes too. PLA that was flexible when new may **snap** when you bend it, and nylon or TPU can feel softer and print with a matte, uneven finish. If a spool printed well last month and badly today with the same settings, moisture is the first thing to check.

## 2. Know Which Filaments Are Most Sensitive

Not every material is equally thirsty. As a rough guide:

| Material | Moisture sensitivity | What wet filament looks like |
|---|---|---|
| PLA | Low to medium | Stringing, brittle strand, rougher surface |
| PETG | Medium | Heavy stringing, blobs, popping |
| ABS / ASA | Medium | Bubbles, rough walls, weaker layers |
| TPU | High | Stringing, bubbles, inconsistent flow |
| Nylon (PA) | Very high | Popping, frothy surface, very weak parts |
| PVA (soluble support) | Very high | Clogs and failed supports, can turn gummy |
| Carbon-fibre-filled blends | Follows the base plastic | Same as the base material |

Nylon and PVA can pick up enough water to print badly within hours of sitting out in a humid room. PLA usually tolerates days or weeks, which is why many people never notice the problem until summer.

## 3. Dry It at the Right Temperature

Drying works by holding the filament warm enough to drive moisture out, but well below the point where it softens and the spool deforms. Always check the temperature printed on your filament’s box first: brands vary. Typical ranges are:

| Material | Typical drying temperature | Typical time |
|---|---|---|
| PLA | 45–50 °C | 4–6 hours |
| PETG | 60–65 °C | 4–6 hours |
| ABS / ASA | 65–80 °C | 4–6 hours |
| TPU | 50–55 °C | 4–8 hours |
| Nylon (PA) | 70–80 °C | 8–12 hours |
| PVA | 45–55 °C | 6–8 hours |

Very wet spools, especially nylon, can need longer. Leave the spool in the dryer to cool slightly before moving it to storage.

**A heated enclosure** can help as well. The BIQU Panda Breath, a chamber heater for Bambu Lab printers, includes a filament-drying mode.

**A kitchen oven** works in a pinch, but be careful. Many ovens overshoot their set temperature by 10 °C or more, which is enough to soften PLA and warp a plastic spool. Use an oven thermometer, keep the door slightly open, and never leave it unattended.

## 5. Store Dry Filament Properly

Drying is wasted if the spool goes straight back onto a shelf. Store spools in **airtight boxes or vacuum bags with desiccant** (silica gel), and add a cheap **hygrometer** so you can see the humidity. Aim to keep the inside of the box below about 20% relative humidity. Recharge or replace the desiccant when the hygrometer starts to climb.

For materials you print a lot, a dry box that feeds straight into the printer keeps the spool dry for the whole of a long print, which matters most for nylon and TPU.

## 6. Make Drying a Habit for the Sensitive Stuff

You don’t need to dry every spool of PLA every time. A sensible routine:

- **Dry new spools of nylon, PVA and TPU before first use**, even if they arrive vacuum sealed.
- **Dry PETG and ABS** whenever stringing or popping appears.
- **Dry PLA** only when it starts to snap or string.
- **Print sensitive materials from a dry box**, not an open spool holder.

## Resin Printers and Moisture

Resin printers don’t use filament, but humidity still matters. Damp, cold air can affect how some resins cure and how well prints stick to the build plate. Keep resin bottles sealed, keep the printer’s room warm, and treat a heated vat, like the one on the ELEGOO Saturn 4 Ultra 16K, as a real advantage in winter.

## The Bottom Line

If your prints suddenly string, pop or snap, dry the filament before you touch a single slicer setting. A filament dryer and a few airtight boxes cost less than a couple of failed long prints, and they make every other printing problem easier to diagnose.
